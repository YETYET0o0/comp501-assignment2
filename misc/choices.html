How Facial Recognition System And CCTV Should Be Used

The use of facial recognition systems and CCTV can benefit retailers in terms of business and security, but also these technologies may raise concerns about privacy, security, and human rights. This section examines the advantages and disadvantages of these technologies to determine how these technologies should be used.



Advantage:

>According to Guo (2025), facial recognition technology is widely used to improve operational efficiency and customer engagement. Furthermore, integrating facial recognition systems with CCTV may help retailers detecting theft, reducing financial losses, and improving the store safety.

>If retails set CCTV and use YOLO, they can count the number of people who visit each corner and how long they stay. This information can help retailers analyze how to make the corners and aisles. Also, if this technology is used for only counting number of people without identifying their biometric information, it may have low privacy risks compared with using facial recognition systems.


Disadvantages:

>Regarding to facial recognition system, Morris (2023) states, “While a highly valuable policing tool, its lack of regulation, technological accuracy and potential racial bias have led some countries, including Aotearoa New Zealand, to impose a moratorium on FRT use in law enforcement.” (p. 1). This means facial recognition system may be difficult to manage safety and responsibly.

>If CCTV is used without facial recognition system, retailers may not be able to store data of each customer without membership or specific way. Also, without facial recognition system or other reliable identification method, retailers may find it difficult to identify a specific person who stole the items.


Three posible approach:

Regarding the choice of retail facial recognition and CCTV under the privacy act in New Zealand, there are three possible approaches.


The first opinion:

The first option is to allow the unrestricted use of facial recognition systems and CCTV in New Zealand.

This option is not ideal because to compare with advantages and disadvantages, facial recognition system is sometimes risky to use. If there are no restriction, the operators may have too much authority. In this case, human rights violation may occur. Additionally, if facial information has stored as data and used to track individual, this technique may contribute to crimes. CCTV also should be restricted because CCTV can record peoples’ outfits, and other identifying characteristics even if facial recognition system or other identification system are not integrated. If these information go to the wrong hands, it might be used to identify individuals without their consent.


The second opinion:

The second option is to allow the use of facial recognition systems and CCTV subject to certain legal restrictions.

This option may be most appropriate because if these techniques are used responsibility, these techniques may be able to protect retailers’ profits and customers safety. However, If the personal information is collected or stored without clear rules and safeguards, this information may be used for unintentional purpose. This could create serious risks to privacy, human right, and security. Nevertheless, if there are appropriate regulation, safety and profits both may be saved.

According to Privacy Commissioner (n.d.), organizations must comply with four rules governing the collection of biometric information (Privacy Commissioner, n.d.). They include requirements relating to the purpose of collection, the source of biometric sample, the collection of information from individual, and the manner of collection of biometric information (Privacy Commissioner, n.d.). To summarize the fact sheet about safety, the data collection must be for lawful purpose, secured properly, and strived to keep the collection minimally and necessary (Privacy Commissioner, n.d.).

In addition, the information must be collected directly from the individual (Privacy Commissioner, n.d.) and when organisation collect the biometrical information, organisations must inform individuals about the purpose of the collection, the fact of the collection, and alternative option. (Privacy Commissioner, n.d.).

These rules can help protect individuals’ privacy while allowing retailers to use CCTV and facial recognition responsibly.


The third option:

The third option is to completely prohibit the use of facial recognition system and CCTV in New Zealand.

This option is not ideal because the techniques offer several benefits for retail business, particularly in preventing theft, improving security, and analysing customer behaviour. Furthermore, as CCTV develops, retailer may be able to automate certain store operations, as demonstrated by Amazon Go. These kinds of developments could help address labour shortages.


Conclusion:

In practice, the second option, which allows the use of facial recognition systems and CCTV subject to appropriate legal restrictions may be the most realistic and appropriate approach.
