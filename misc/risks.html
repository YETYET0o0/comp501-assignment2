Technology Risks

Risk 1: Invasion of Privacy

Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV) and Facial Recognition Technology (FRT) represent significant opportunities for enhanced security. However, these same technologies present a considerable risk to an individual's right to privacy. When organisations utilize these technologies, it is possible that information will be gathered about individuals who do not commit any crimes. For example, people may be recorded in supermarkets, shopping centres and at sporting events without fully understanding how their personal information is being collected and used.

Hughes (2012) describes privacy as the respect for boundaries established through social interaction. According to Hughes (2012), privacy can be significant in public areas, and individuals cannot always avoid being recorded via closed-circuit television (p. 814). This suggests that entering a public space does not necessarily mean that individuals lose their expectations of privacy. As surveillance technologies continue to evolve, individuals may have less control over how their images and personal information are collected.

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner (n.d.) states that organisations utilizing CCTV must have a valid reason for the collection of personal information and must only collect the information required. Combining CCTV with FRT may increase privacy concerns because FRT can analyse facial characteristics to identify individuals. For example, Foodstuffs North Island's FRT trial processed approximately 226 million facial scans between February and September 2024 (Office of the Privacy Commissioner, 2025). Although almost all facial scans were deleted within one minute, this example highlights the large amount of personal information that facial recognition systems can process.

Although the use of CCTV or FRT does not automatically result in violations of privacy laws, the level of risk depends on how the technology is utilized, how much information is collected and what measures are taken to protect the individual's right to privacy. Therefore, organisations that intend to introduce or expand surveillance systems need to take into account the potential implications on individual privacy.

Risk 2: Misidentification and Algorithmic Bias

Misidentification and algorithmic bias are two major risks associated with the use of Facial Recognition Technology (FRT). Although FRT can automatically compare facial images captured by security cameras with an existing watchlist, the technology is not always accurate. A false positive occurs when the system incorrectly matches an innocent person to someone on a watchlist. This may lead to unnecessary security checks, embarrassment, or unfair treatment.

According to Raposo (2024), facial recognition technology identifies suspects through probabilities and does not guarantee complete certainty. Therefore, there is the potential for errors in identity. Furthermore, Raposo (2024) states that while many facial recognition technologies appear to be neutral, some algorithms exhibit disparate accuracy levels based upon ethnicity and/or gender. In other words, the likelihood of false positives may vary depending upon the demographics of the subjects being evaluated; thus, certain populations may experience greater exposure to misidentifications due to the particular technology being utilised.

In New Zealand, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner (2025) found misidentification and technological bias to be important areas of concern regarding the Foodstuffs North Island FRT trial. The Commissioner stated that the trial had not fully addressed concerns about technical bias, including its potential negative impact on Māori and Pacific people. As such, organisations should seriously consider the equity and accuracy of FRT prior to implementing it in retail establishments like supermarkets and shopping malls.

This is particularly true for larger public events where there may be thousands of visitors attending concerts. An error resulting in an innocent attendee being stopped could significantly alter the attendee’s experience. However, not all FRT systems have the same level of accuracy. The risk of errors depends on factors such as the accuracy of the FRT system, the quality of facial images and whether staff verify alerts before taking further action (Raposo, 2024).

Risk 3: Access and Misuse of CCTV Footage

An additional concern related to the use of closed-circuit television (CCTV) is the unauthorised access or dissemination of video footage. Video captured through CCTV can include many people at once in places like supermarkets, shopping centers and events open to the general public. Although the footage may be collected for security purposes, it can also contain personal information about individuals who are not involved in any security incidents.

Öztürk (2017) discusses the privacy concerns associated with law enforcement agencies accessing CCTV footage recorded by private organisations. The author points out that access would not just affect people under investigation, but also the others caught on camera. Thus, distributing recorded surveillance footage can create added risk even though the primary intention for collecting this footage was as a means to enhance safety.

In New Zealand, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner (n.d.) explains that organisations using CCTV should consider how recorded footage is stored, protected and accessed. For example a retailer or organiser of an event review CCTV footage after a security incident has occurred. However, if the video record is distributed further than required or accessed without proper authorisation, customers' personal data could be compromised or mishandled.
